The federal government of the United States recently endured the longest shutdown in the nation’s history, halting many operations for 43 days before a last-minute bipartisan deal reopened agencies in mid-November 2025. The funding lapse began after Congress failed to agree on spending bills for the 2026 fiscal year, forcing hundreds of thousands of federal workers to go without pay and disrupting services across the country.​
How the shutdown began
The shutdown started on October 1, 2025, when Congress missed the deadline to pass appropriations or a temporary funding measure for the new fiscal year. A Republican-controlled House backed a continuing resolution that stalled in the Senate, where Democrats demanded the extension of enhanced Affordable Care Act subsidies as a condition for their support.​
Political standoff in Washington
For weeks, the Senate repeatedly voted on rival funding proposals, with neither side able to clear the 60-vote threshold needed to break a filibuster. The dispute centered on health insurance subsidies and spending priorities, with each party accusing the other of using federal workers and key programs as leverage.​
Impact on agencies and workers
Large numbers of federal employees were furloughed or working without pay, though “essential” functions such as air traffic control, law enforcement, and certain benefit payments continued. Some safety-net programs faced growing strain, with warnings over delayed nutrition assistance and pressures on state budgets that rely on federal funds.​
The deal that ended the shutdown
The deadlock broke when the Senate approved a compromise funding package on November 10 by a 60–40 vote, followed by House passage on November 12. President Donald Trump signed the bill the same day, restoring funding through January 30 and providing full-year appropriations for veterans’ programs, Congress, and the Department of Agriculture while scheduling a later Senate vote on healthcare subsidies.​
Aftermath and political fallout
Because the shutdown lasted 43 days, it surpassed all previous funding gaps and has already become a central issue in debates over Congress’s ability to govern. Early analyses suggest the crisis damaged the public image of Republicans in Congress and is being cited as a factor in recent Democratic gains in several 2025 state and local elections.​
